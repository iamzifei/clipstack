ClipStack for macOS

Everything you copied is still there. ⇧⌘V

macOS remembers exactly one thing you copied. ClipStack keeps the rest — text, images and files — in a searchable panel that opens over whatever you are doing, previews the full contents, and puts the one you want back on the clipboard with a single keystroke.

Real history, invented on purpose: every entry in this recording is demo data.

A clipboard that keeps up with you.

Copy three things in a row and all three are waiting — not just the last one.

Search it, do not scroll it

Start typing and the list narrows as you go, Chinese input included. Arrow keys move the selection, ⌘1–9 grabs one of the first nine outright, and Return copies the selected entry and closes the panel.

Know what each entry is at a glance

Every row carries a tag — TEXT, URL, EMAIL, CODE, COLOR, IMAGE, FILE — so you can spot the link or the snippet by its shape before reading a word. A copied colour shows its swatch, with HEX, RGB and HSL side by side in the preview.

Only the links. Only the code.

A row of chips under the search field counts each type in your history. Tab steps to the next one and ⇧Tab back, or click a chip; it combines with search, and esc clears it. Handy when the URL you want is buried under forty snippets.

Fix it before you paste it

⌘K opens a menu built for the selected entry: change case, trim, join lines, format or minify JSON, URL-encode or decode. A colour converts between HEX, RGB and HSL, and a screenshot gives up its text through on-device OCR. The result is copied and kept as a new entry.

Several pieces, one paste

⌘M or ⌘-click marks entries, and each mark shows its number. Return then copies them all as one block, one per line, in the order you marked them.

Lift one entry onto the screen

⌘O turns the selected entry into its own small window that floats above everything. Open as many as you need, drag them next to the form you are filling in, and copy from each with one click.

Text, images and files

Rich text keeps its formatting, images stay images, and files copied in Finder come back as files. The preview pane on the right shows the whole thing before you commit to it — not a truncated line.

Keep it on screen while you work

⌘T pins the panel above every window and stops it closing when it loses focus or after a copy. It becomes a reference card you read from while typing somewhere else — the state survives a restart.

The ones you keep reusing, pinned

⌘P pins an entry to the top of the list and out of the way of the 300-entry limit, so the snippet you paste ten times a day never falls off the end.

Every key, and where it goes.

  • ⇧⌘VOpen the panel over whatever app you are in
  • ↩Copy the selected entry and close
  • ⌘1–9Copy one of the first nine directly
  • ⌘PPin or unpin the selected entry
  • ⌘TKeep the panel on top and open
  • ⌘⌫Delete the selected entry
  • ⇧⌘⌫Clear the whole history, after a confirmation
  • ⇥⇧⇥Filter by type — step through All, TEXT, URL, CODE and the rest
  • ⌘KTransform the selected entry — case, JSON, URL encoding, colour formats, OCR
  • ⌘MMark entries (or ⌘-click), then Return copies them merged
  • ⌘OOpen the selected entry as a floating note
  • ⌘VVOptional: hold ⌘ and tap V to step back through history, release to paste
  • ⌘,Settings — launch at login, light, dark or system appearance, and all three shortcuts are re-recordable

It stays on your Mac

History lives in a plain folder in your Library and goes nowhere else. There is no account, no sync, no analytics and no network call except the update check.

Anything a password manager marks as concealed or transient is never recorded, following the nspasteboard.org convention — so the password you just copied does not end up in a list.

Out of the box ClipStack needs no permissions at all. Two optional settings — paste straight into the app you came from, and hold ⌘ and tap V to cycle history — use macOS Accessibility, and nothing asks for it until you switch one on.
